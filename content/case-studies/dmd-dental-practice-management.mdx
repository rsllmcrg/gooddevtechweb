## The problem

A dental clinic runs on information that arrives in the wrong order. The
patient shows up before their chart is open, the medical history is a paper
form filled in at the counter, the insurance card is at home, and the waiting
room has no idea whose turn it is — so the front desk answers "how much longer?"
instead of doing the work in front of them.

Off-the-shelf practice management software solves some of that and imposes a
lot: per-seat pricing, workflows built for a different country's insurance
system, and no room for how this clinic actually greets people. The clinic
needed the whole path — arrival, chart, treatment, invoice — to be one system
rather than a scheduling tool plus a spreadsheet plus a paper folder.

## The approach

The build treats the clinic as two audiences with different needs, sharing one
data model.

- **A kiosk the patient can use unaided.** Arrival starts with one question —
  first visit, or returning — and branches from there. New patients complete
  their own intake, including the full medical history, allergy, and medication
  questionnaire, in language that reads like a person wrote it. Returning
  patients just identify themselves and join the queue.
- **A waiting room that answers its own question.** A live display carries the
  number being served, how many are ahead, and an approximate wait, refreshed
  without anyone touching it. The front desk stops being a status API.
- **Clinical records built around the tooth, not the appointment.** Charting
  works per tooth and per surface, with the conditions a dentist actually
  records — caries, crown, implant, denture — feeding treatment plan items
  priced off a configurable fee schedule.
- **Insurance and billing modelled properly.** Carrier and HMO plans carry
  group ID, effective date, deductible, copay, and annual maximum, so
  eligibility is checked before treatment rather than discovered at the
  counter. Invoices age into 0–30 / 31–60 / 61–90 / 90+ buckets, which is how
  receivables are actually chased.
- **Configuration instead of code changes.** Operatories, practitioners,
  clinic hours, default appointment length, and default fees are all settings.
  A second chair or a new dentist is data entry, not a deployment.
- **Roles that match the room.** Dentist, hygienist, assistant, and patient see
  different applications built from the same API — the patient's kiosk can't
  reach a chart, and doesn't need to.

Built for a Filipino clinic, so the intake reads bilingually where it matters
— the "no known conditions" answer is a button that says _Healthy po ako!_
rather than a negation of a checklist.

## The outcome

The clinic runs one system from the front door to the invoice. A patient
checks themselves in, the waiting room tells them where they stand, and the
chart is open before they sit down — while scheduling, clinical notes,
prescriptions, treatment plans, insurance, and receivables all read from the
same records instead of being reconciled by hand.

Because operatories, practitioners, hours, and fees are configuration rather
than assumptions baked into the code, the same system fits the next clinic
without a rewrite.
